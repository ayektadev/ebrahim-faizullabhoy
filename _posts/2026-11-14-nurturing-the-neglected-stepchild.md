In 2008, the physicians Paul Farmer and Jim Yong Kim wrote a short essay in the *World Journal of Surgery* with a line that has followed the field ever since. "In Africa," they wrote, "surgery may be thought of as the neglected stepchild of global public health."<sup>1</sup> Farmer later dropped the qualifier and put it more simply: surgery is the neglected stepchild of global health.

It is a striking phrase, partly because surgery is so obviously central to medicine everywhere else. Nobody in Atlanta or New York would describe an appendectomy, a cesarean section, or the repair of a broken leg as a luxury. Yet for most of the modern history of global health, the operating room sat at the edge of the conversation.

## The size of the gap

The clearest accounting came in 2015, when the *Lancet* Commission on Global Surgery published its report. Its first finding was that five billion people do not have access to safe, affordable surgical and anaesthesia care when they need it.<sup>2</sup> That is roughly two of every three people alive.

The commission estimated that 143 million additional surgical procedures are needed in low- and middle-income countries each year just to save lives and prevent disability. Of the roughly 313 million operations performed worldwide each year, only 6% take place in the poorest countries, where more than a third of the world's population lives.<sup>2</sup>

The human cost is not abstract. Nearly a third of all deaths in 2010, about 16.9 million, came from conditions treatable with surgery, more than HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria combined.<sup>3</sup> Cost is its own kind of harm, too. Each year, 33 million people face catastrophic health expenditure paying for surgery and anaesthesia, and a quarter of people who have an operation are pushed into financial catastrophe by it.<sup>2</sup>

## The weight of a harvest

Numbers that large can hide what a single untreated condition does to a single life, so it helps to look at one. Take the inguinal hernia, one of the most common operations in the world and one of the least glamorous.

In Ghana, where the International Surgical Health Initiative has operated alongside Ghanaian colleagues at Tetteh Quarshie Memorial Hospital since 2009, researchers estimated that new inguinal hernias appear at a rate of about 210 per 100,000 people each year, while only about 30 per 100,000 are repaired. Over a decade, that gap leaves roughly a million people waiting. Clearing the backlog would cost an estimated $53 million and avert around five million disability-adjusted life years.<sup>4</sup>

For a farmer in rural Ghana, a hernia is not merely a surgical diagnosis. It is a slow tax on the body that does the work. The bulge grows with every season of lifting and bending, and with it the pain, the lost days in the field, and the quiet fear that one day it will strangle and become an emergency far from any operating room. A repair takes under an hour. Its absence can cost a family its harvest, its income, and sometimes its provider.

That is the part of global surgery that rarely makes the headlines. We tend to judge impact by complexity, by the hardest case or the most dramatic save. But a one-hour operation that returns a farmer to his field is, measured by what it gives back to a household, one of the most powerful things medicine can do.

## Why surgery was left out

Farmer and Kim offered a few explanations. International health had been organized for decades around communicable disease, from smallpox to AIDS. Few surgeons were involved in global health policy. And there was no equivalent of the Global Fund for surgical care, so the money and attention followed the diseases that already had champions.<sup>1</sup>

There was also a perception problem. Surgery was seen as expensive, specialized, and hospital-bound, the kind of care a health system could add later, once the basics were in place. The commission's economic work argued the opposite: that essential surgery is cost-effective and that the cost of *not* providing it is far higher.<sup>2</sup>

I think there is a simpler reason, too. Surgery is hard to deliver without a working system around it. An operation needs a trained surgeon and an anaesthetist, but it also needs oxygen, blood, sterile instruments, electricity, a recovery bed, someone to check the wound a week later, and a record of what happened. You cannot run a vertical surgery program the way you can distribute bed nets. When surgery fails in a place, it is usually because some other part of the health system failed first.

That is also why I find surgery such a useful lens. If a hospital can reliably take a patient from diagnosis through operation to follow-up, almost everything else in that system is working too.

## Follow the money

The neglect shows up most plainly in the funding. The largest funders of global health, the Gates Foundation chief among them, have put extraordinary sums into vaccines, HIV, malaria, and maternal and child health, and those investments have saved millions of lives. Surgery has not shared in that. When researchers tried to track it, they found that less than 1% of the development assistance for health flowing from high-income to low- and middle-income countries in 2011 went to surgery.<sup>6</sup> The condition behind nearly a third of the world's deaths receives a rounding error.

How the money moves matters as much as how much there is. Over the past two decades, much of global health has tried, imperfectly, to build independence: national programs, local governance, domestic financing, and health workers trained to run their own systems. Global surgery has often moved the other way. Too much of it still arrives as short visits from teams based in high-income countries, funded by those countries' NGOs and agencies, and gone when the mission ends.

At its worst, the arrangement is almost feudal. Hospitals come to depend on the visiting team for equipment, for expertise, and for the operations themselves, and that dependence becomes the condition of getting care at all. The host surgeons do the follow-up, absorb the complications, and keep the system running between visits, yet the data, the publications, and the decisions tend to travel home with the visitors.

It should be a partnership in both directions. Surgeons in Kabala or Accra know things about their patients, their constraints, and their outcomes that no visiting team can learn in two weeks, and surgeons from high-income countries have a great deal to learn from how care is delivered with far less. A relationship like that is built on shared data, shared authorship, and a plan for the host program to need the visitors less each year, not more.

## What has changed

The past decade has moved surgery closer to the center. In 2015, the World Health Assembly passed resolution 68.15, recognizing emergency and essential surgical care and anaesthesia as part of universal health coverage.<sup>7</sup> The *Lancet* commission gave countries a set of indicators to track and a template for national surgical plans, and a growing number of governments have since written their own.<sup>2</sup>

Still, attention is not the same as access. Much of the surgery delivered in low-resource settings comes through short-term missions, and the outcomes of those operations often go unrecorded once the visiting team leaves. In my own research, recovering twelve years of handwritten records from one hospital in Sierra Leone surfaced complications that had never made it into the mission's case logs. If outcomes are invisible, it is hard to argue that the work matters, and harder still to improve it, or to hand it over.

## Why it matters

The stepchild metaphor works because it describes a family that has the resources to care for everyone and chooses not to. Global health has learned to treat infections at enormous scale. The same ambition can include the patient with an obstructed labor, a strangulated hernia, or a crushed limb after a road crash.

Surgery is not something a health system earns once everything else is in place. In many ways, it is how you can tell whether the rest of the system is there at all.
