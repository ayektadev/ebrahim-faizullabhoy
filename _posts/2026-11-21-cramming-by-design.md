The name came first. Cramium is a mash-up of *cram* and *cranium*, which is pretty much what the first two years of medical school feel like: an absurd amount of material going into your head in not much time. I wanted a tool that treated that as a real problem to solve instead of something you're supposed to just grind through.

I started building it as an M1, and the idea was simple. Most of what we study comes from our own lecture slides, but most study tools ignore them. You either spend hours making flashcards by hand or grab a premade deck that covers half of what your school tests and plenty that it doesn't. I wanted to upload a lecture once and get study material that was tied to that lecture and nothing else.

## What it does

You upload slides, PDFs, or notes, and Cramium builds a study guide, flashcards, and board-style practice questions from them. Every card and question links back to the slide it came from, so when you get something wrong you can see exactly where it was taught. A tutor called Pearl answers questions about your own uploads, and a planning engine decides what you should review next based on what you have missed, how long it has been, and how many days you have until the exam.

## How I use it

My own routine is a lot simpler than the feature list. After lecture, I upload the day's slides and do a short set of questions that night. On days with no new lectures, I review a lecture from three to seven days back. Before an exam, I let the planner pick my sessions instead of guessing which topics I'm weakest on. For boards-style practice I still lean on UWorld; Cramium covers the school-specific material that question banks don't.

## Building and studying at the same time

Building a company in medical school is hard, and I underestimated how hard. During the stretch when I was most locked in on Cramium, my grades slipped a little, which is about as ironic as it gets: I was building a study tool and studying less because of it.

What kept it from becoming a real problem was a phrase from Dr. George Heinrich, the longtime associate dean of admissions at NJMS, who passed away in the summer of 2025, shortly before my class arrived. He reminded students not to forget their "day job." For me, the day job is medicine. I care a lot about Cramium, but I'm here to become a doctor, and when the two compete, medicine wins. These days I block off study time first and build around it. The company moves a little slower, and I'm fine with that.

## Competition, and a hard lesson

The AI study tool space filled up fast. A few competitors (ahem, Ora AI) came in at prices that are hard for a two-person team to match.

Early on, I reached out to one of those teams for mentorship. I was new, I liked what they'd built, and I was a lot more open than I should have been about how our adaptive notes worked. Not long after, a very similar feature showed up on their end. I can't say how they built it, but I learned my lesson: get advice from people who aren't building the same thing as you, and keep your core tech to yourself until it's protected.

## The tradeoffs

Building Cramium has taught me more about software, design, and how people learn than any class could have. I have a co-founder I trust, a product students actually use, and a much better sense of what I'm good at. It has also cost me sleep, a few exam points, and some of my naivety about the startup world.

I'd do it again. I'd just start protecting my study time on day one instead of month six.
